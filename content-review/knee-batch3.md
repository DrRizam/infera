## 1. `kn3-r1` — ACL & instability (recognition)

*Multiple choice* · Status: **🔍 SOURCE-CHECKED (figures re-checked against the cited paper on 2026-08-04; your clinical sign-off still needed)**

**Stem:** With the patient supine and the knee flexed to roughly 20-30°, the examiner stabilises the femur with one hand and draws the tibia anteriorly with the other, feeling for the quality of the end point. Which test is this?

**Correct answer:** Lachman test

**Distractors:** Anterior drawer test · Pivot shift test · Posterior sag sign

**Explanation (shown after answering):** The Lachman is performed at 20-30° of flexion, unlike the anterior drawer at 90°. What you are grading is not just translation but the END POINT: a soft or absent end point is the finding, and it is why the test survives even when the patient is guarding. The lower flexion angle also relaxes the hamstrings, which is a large part of why it outperforms the anterior drawer in acute knees.

**Pearl:** Lachman at 20-30°, anterior drawer at 90°. If you are testing at 90° in a swollen acute knee, hamstring guarding will hide the tear.

> 📚 **Citation:** Benjaminse A, Gokeler A, van der Schans CP. Clinical diagnosis of an anterior cruciate ligament rupture: a meta-analysis. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther. 2006;36(5):267-288.

## 3. `kn3-r3` — Patellofemoral pain (recognition)

*Discriminate two conditions* · Status: **UNVERIFIED**

**Stem:** A 24-year-old runner reports anterior knee pain. Which reported feature points toward PATELLOFEMORAL PAIN rather than a meniscal tear?

**Correct answer:** Pain on prolonged sitting, stairs, and squatting, with no history of a twisting injury or true locking

**Distractors:** A twisting injury followed by delayed swelling over 24 hours · Intermittent true locking that the patient has to manoeuvre out of · Well-localised joint-line pain reproduced by deep flexion and rotation

**Explanation (shown after answering):** Patellofemoral pain is typically insidious, diffuse or peri-patellar, and provoked by loaded knee flexion — stairs, squatting, running, and prolonged sitting (the so-called theatre sign). Meniscal pathology more often follows a twisting mechanism, produces joint-line pain, and may cause true mechanical locking. **Pearl:** Ask about prolonged sitting. Anterior knee pain that hates sitting still is far more patellofemoral than meniscal.

## 4. `kn3-r4` — Knee measurement (recognition)

*Multiple choice* · Status: **UNVERIFIED**

**Stem:** The examiner strokes upward along the medial side of the knee to empty the medial gutter, then strokes down the lateral side and watches the medial side for a returning bulge of fluid. What is being assessed?

**Correct answer:** A small joint effusion (sweep or stroke test)

**Distractors:** Patellar tendon integrity · Medial collateral ligament laxity · A Baker's cyst

## 5. `kn3-p1` — ACL & instability (procedure)

*Order the steps / rank* · Status: **🔍 SOURCE-CHECKED (figures re-checked against the cited paper on 2026-08-04; your clinical sign-off still needed)**

**Stem:** Order the steps for performing the Lachman test.

**Correct order:**
1. Position the patient supine with the limb relaxed and explain what you will do
2. Support the knee in about 20-30° of flexion with slight external tibial rotation
3. Stabilise the femur with the outer hand and grasp the proximal tibia with the inner hand
4. Draw the tibia anteriorly with a brisk, controlled force
5. Grade the amount of translation AND the quality of the end point, comparing with the other knee

**Explanation (shown after answering):** Relaxation is the whole game — a guarding hamstring can mask a complete rupture, which is why the unaffected side is tested first in practice and why the knee is supported rather than held rigidly. The end point matters as much as the excursion: a torn ACL classically gives increased translation with a soft or mushy end feel.

> 📚 **Citation:** Benjaminse A, Gokeler A, van der Schans CP. J Orthop Sports Phys Ther. 2006;36(5):267-288 (test description and pooled accuracy).

## 6. `kn3-p2` — Meniscus (procedure)

*Order the steps / rank* · Status: **UNVERIFIED**

**Stem:** Order the steps of the McMurray test for a medial meniscal tear.

**Correct order:**
1. Position the patient supine and fully flex the hip and knee
2. Grasp the heel with one hand and palpate the medial joint line with the other
3. Externally rotate the tibia and apply a valgus force
4. Extend the knee smoothly while maintaining rotation and valgus
5. Record a positive only if a palpable or audible click occurs WITH the patient's familiar joint-line pain

**Explanation (shown after answering):** The positive criterion is what most examiners get wrong. A click alone is common in asymptomatic knees; the finding that carries any diagnostic weight is a click or thud accompanied by the patient's familiar pain at the joint line. For the lateral meniscus the rotation and force are reversed (internal rotation with varus).

**Pearl:** Painless clicks are noise. Record what the patient felt, not just what you heard.

> 📚 **Citation:** Smith BE, Thacker D, Crewesmith A, Hall M. Special tests for assessing meniscal tears within the knee: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Evid Based Med. 2015;20(3):88-97 (accuracy); test description per Magee DJ. Orthopedic Physical Assessment. 6th ed. Elsevier Saunders; 2014.

## 7. `kn3-p3` — Knee measurement (procedure)

*Order the steps / rank* · Status: **UNVERIFIED**

**Stem:** Order the steps for goniometric measurement of knee flexion.

**Correct order:**
1. Position the patient supine with the hip and knee in neutral extension
2. Centre the goniometer fulcrum over the lateral epicondyle of the femur
3. Align the stationary arm with the greater trochanter and the moving arm with the lateral malleolus
4. Flex the knee through available range, allowing the hip to flex with it
5. Read the angle at the end of available range

**Explanation (shown after answering):** Position, fulcrum, alignment, motion, measurement. Allowing hip flexion matters: restricting it puts rectus femoris on stretch and will under-read knee flexion in a two-joint muscle. Record whether the measurement was active or passive, and be consistent between visits — mixing the two is a common source of apparent 'progress'.

> 📚 **Citation:** Norkin CC, White DJ. Measurement of Joint Motion: A Guide to Goniometry. 5th ed. F.A. Davis; 2016 (knee flexion procedure).

## 8. `kn3-p4` — Knee red flags (procedure)

*Order the steps / rank* · Status: **🔍 SOURCE-CHECKED (figures re-checked against the cited paper on 2026-08-04; your clinical sign-off still needed)**

**Stem:** A patient presents within a day of a knee injury. Order the steps for applying the Ottawa Knee Rule.

**Correct order:**
1. Confirm the rule applies: acute knee trauma, not an isolated superficial wound or a re-assessment of a known fracture
2. Ask the patient's age and check whether they are 55 or older
3. Palpate for isolated tenderness of the patella and for tenderness at the head of the fibula
4. Test whether the patient can flex the knee to 90 degrees
5. Ask the patient to take four weight-bearing steps, both immediately after injury and now
6. Refer for radiography if any one criterion is met; if none are met, imaging is not indicated

**Explanation (shown after answering):** The five criteria are age 55 or over, isolated patellar tenderness, fibular head tenderness, inability to flex to 90°, and inability to bear weight for four steps both immediately and at assessment. ANY single positive triggers imaging. The rule is designed to be over-inclusive — it exists to safely rule fracture OUT, not to predict who has one.

**Pearl:** The Ottawa rules are a rule-out tool. A positive doesn't mean fracture; it means you can't skip the X-ray.

> 📚 **Citation:** Stiell IG, Greenberg GH, Wells GA, et al. Prospective validation of a decision rule for the use of radiography in acute knee injuries. JAMA. 1996;275(8):611-615; pooled accuracy per Bachmann LM, et al. Ann Intern Med. 2004;140(2):121-124.

*Multiple choice* · Status: **🔍 SOURCE-CHECKED (figures re-checked against the cited paper on 2026-08-04; your clinical sign-off still needed)**

**Stem:** In the same review, requiring BOTH the Thessaly and McMurray tests to be positive gave sensitivity around 53% and specificity around 62% — worse than either test alone. What is the lesson?

**Correct answer:** Combining tests does not automatically improve accuracy; a combination has to be validated as a combination

**Distractors:** Test combinations always outperform single tests · It proves both tests are worthless · It means the tests should be performed in the opposite order
